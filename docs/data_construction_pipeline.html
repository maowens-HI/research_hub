Data Construction Pipeline

Building the District, Tract, and County Panels for School Finance Analysis

Author

Myles Owens

Published

January 22, 2026

0.1 Overview

This document explains how we build analysis-ready datasets from raw school finance data. The pipeline transforms unbalanced district-level spending records into a balanced county-year panel suitable for event-study analysis of school finance reforms.

The Pipeline at a Glance:

Stage Purpose
1 Unify historical and modern district finance data
2 Assign districts to Census tracts
3 Flag counties with complete baseline data
4 Aggregate tracts to counties, merge reforms
5 Apply sample restrictions, create analysis variables
6 Run event-study regressions

1 Stage 1: District Panel Construction

Stage 1 unifies historical and modern school finance data into a single district-year panel. The key challenge in unification is that historical data and modern data use two different district identifier systems. The historical data from the Census’ Individual Government Finance Database (INDFIN) uses the Government ID standard (GOVID) while modern data from the National Center for Education Statistics (NCES) Local Education Agency Finance Survey (f-33) uses the Local Education Agency ID standard (LEAID). Fortunately, we are able to construct a crosswalk from the f-33 data as it includes links between each LEAID to each GOVID. This crosswalk enables us to append historical and modern data, and ultimately link everything to Census geography for county-level aggregation.

This seems like it would be a straightforward process, but there is a major caveat: each LEAID does not always have a one-to-one match in the GOVID system. We will elaborate further in the subsequent section.

Linking District Identifiers

Source Years Identifier Coverage

INDFIN (Individual Government Finance Database) 1967–1991 GOVID (Government ID) Historical finance data

1.2 Step 1: Build the LEAID↔︎GOVID Crosswalk

Not every LEAID maps to exactly one GOVID. There are a variety of reasons why this can happen. Districts split, merge, and change codes over time, creating one-to-many and many-to-many relationships. However, there is a conservative solution to this problem: we keep only the clean one-to-one pairs. Concretely, this means building a dataset of only districts where one LEAID corresponds to exactly one GOVID across all years.

Why drop non-one-to-one relationships? Ambiguous mappings prevent us from consistently tracking a district over time. If LEAID “A” maps to both GOVID “X” and GOVID “Y” in different years, we cannot reliably link that district’s historical and modern spending data.

After filtering, we retain 15,047 clean one-to-one pairs in the xwalk_leaid_govid crosswalk.

1.3 Step 2: Add LEAIDs to INDFIN Panel

As stated previously, the INDFIN panel contains historical spending data identified only by GOVID. To link it with modern data and Census geography, we must assign LEAIDs to each GOVID. The first step is to get a list of every GOVID in INDFIN. To do this, we collapse the INDFIN panel by GOVID to create govtag, giving us a file with one row per GOVID. This sets us up to use our crosswalk to assign LEAIDs.

Remember that our crosswalk only contains GOVIDs and LEAIDs with one-to-one relationships. Therefore, we expect a portion of IDs from govtag to go unmatched. The merge attaches quality flags from INDFIN to the crosswalk pairs. After dropping unmatched GOVIDs (those not in the crosswalk), we retain the full 15,047 LEAID-GOVID pairs from the crosswalk. Of these, 13,773 have INDFIN spending data and inherit their quality flags; the remaining 1,274 crosswalk pairs have no INDFIN data, so their flags are set to zero.

1.3.1 Baseline year flags

Before we move on to the next step of our data cleaning process we must first mention another important step concerning the INDFIN panel and govtag. During this part in the pipeline, before we collapse INDFIN into govtag, we create flags indicating whether each GOVID has valid spending in what we call the baseline spending years (1967, 1970, 1971, 1972). The name stems from the fact that we are concerned with the spending of each district during the beginning period of INDFIN, before the exogenous shocks central to our research design occurred.

Baseline spending plays a critical role in our event-study analysis. The event-study design compares how reforms affected districts with different initial spending levels. Districts in the bottom quartile (Q1) had the lowest pre-reform spending; districts in the top quartile (Q4) had the highest. Without baseline spending data, we cannot classify districts into quartiles and cannot test for heterogeneous treatment effects.

If a district lacks valid spending data for the baseline years, we cannot include it in the regression. The quality flags (good_govid_1967, good_govid_1970, etc.) track this. These flags propagate through the pipeline, from districts to tracts to counties, ensuring our final sample contains only observations with complete baseline histories.

This explains why we retain the crosswalk-only pairs from the previous merge. Those pairs have valid LEAIDs, so they can still be linked to census tracts. Their flags propagate through the pipeline like any other observation. But since their flags are set to zero, they will be excluded from analyses requiring baseline spending.

1.4 Step 3: Restrict F-33 Panel to Crosswalk

Steps 2 and 3 are parallel operations on opposite ends of our panel. Step 2 attached LEAIDs to historical INDFIN data (1967–1991); Step 3 restricts modern F-33 data (1992–2022) to the same set of clean identifiers.

Without this restriction, we would include districts that we cannot reliably track across the historical/modern data boundary. For example, if a district’s LEAID maps to multiple GOVIDs, we cannot determine which historical spending record (if any) corresponds to the modern district. By restricting to the crosswalk, we ensure that every district in our final panel has a consistent identifier across all years.

As with the data set in step 2, any observation in the F33 panel whose LEAID does not appear in the crosswalk gets dropped. This leaves us with 15,047 district-year observations, all of which share identifiers with our restricted historical data. The two panels are now ready to be appended into a single district-year panel spanning 1967–2022.

Data limitations to note:

The panel is unbalanced: not every district appears in every year due to districts entering or exiting the data over time. The quality flags from Step 2 track which districts have valid baseline spending. Additionally, INDFIN contains no data for 1968 or 1969, creating a gap between 1967 and 1970.

1.6 Step 5: Link to Census Geography (GRF Merge)

The final step of Stage 1 connects our district finance data to Census geography. The Geographic Reference File (GRF) from 1969 provides the link between school district boundaries (identified by LEAID) and 1970 Census tracts. This geographic linkage is essential for three reasons.

First, our analysis occurs at the county level. While the original JJP study was at the district level, our replication analyzes spending at the county level. Counties provide a stable geographic unit over time since county boundaries change rarely, and they align well with the administrative data we have access to.

Second, tracts nest cleanly within counties; every tract belongs to exactly one county. By linking districts to tracts, and then tracts to counties in Stage 2, we can compute county-level spending measures through straightforward aggregation.

Third, the GRF provides consistent geographic definitions over time. The 1969 GRF captures district boundaries as of the 1969–70 school year, which means districts that formed later, dissolved earlier, or underwent major reorganization may not appear. We accept this limitation because it mirrors the approach used in JJP and gives us a stable geographic foundation for the entire panel.

With these motivations in mind, we merge the district panel with the GRF on LEAID:

Understanding the merge results

The merge drops a substantial number of observations on both sides, so it’s worth understanding what we’re losing and why.

The 2,932 master-only records represent districts that appear in the GRF but have no finance data in our panel. These districts may have had non-1:1 LEAID-GOVID mappings (and were therefore dropped in Step 1) or may have been small or short-lived districts not consistently tracked in the finance data.

The 5,611 using-only districts are districts from our finance panel that we cannot link to Census geography. This may occur because these districts operated in areas not covered by the 1969 GRF, either because they formed after 1969 or because their boundaries did not contain any tracts in that year’s geographic file.

These dropped observations could potentially be recovered at a later stage if we locate alternative geographic crosswalks. This may be important if we later determine that the coverage gaps are bias our analysis.

Understanding the merge results

Of the 12,368 unique LEAIDs in the GRF, 9,428 (76%) successfully match our district panel. The resulting canonical panel contains only districts that meet all three criteria: (1) they have clean 1:1 LEAID↔︎GOVID mappings, (2) they appear in the 1969 GRF, and (3) they have spending data for at least some years. This is our cleanest, most consistently-defined sample of districts, and it forms the foundation for the tract and county panels we construct in subsequent stages.

2 Stage 2: Tract Panel Construction

Stage 1 produced a district-year panel with consistent identifiers and quality flags. Stage 2 transforms this into a tract-year panel, an intermediate step essential for constructing county-level spending measures.

2.0.1 The Core Challenge

The complication is that multiple districts can serve the same tract. In many areas, separate districts handle primary and secondary schools. Some tracts fall within overlapping district boundaries. For clean county aggregation, we need exactly one spending value per tract.

Our solution is to assign each tract to the district serving the largest share of its population. The 1969 Geographic Reference File records population allocations between tracts and districts. We sum these allocations and pick the “winning” district for each tract. The following sections walk through this process in detail.

Dataset Purpose
GRF 1969 Links tracts to districts with population weights

2.2 Key Operations

2.2.1 Step 1: GRF Import and Cleaning

The first step prepares the Geographic Reference File for use. We import the raw GRF, drop special geographies (water bodies, Native lands, ships, tract revisions), and construct standardized identifiers.

ID Components Length Example
tract70 state(2) + county(3) + btc(4) + tsc(2) 11 chars 01001000100
LEAID state(2) + sdc(5) 7 chars 0100005

Here btc = basic tract code, tsc = tract suffix code, and sdc = school district code.

2.2.2 Understanding Allocated Population

A key concept in the GRF is “allocated population.” The GRF records how much of each block’s population each district serves. A single block can be split across multiple districts.

The formula is:

\[ \text{alloc\_pop} = \text{popc} \times \frac{\text{perc}}{100} \]

where popc is block population and perc is the percentage served by the district.

Example: Consider a tract with two blocks:

Tract Block popc District perc alloc_pop
001 A 1,000 District 1 60% 600
001 A 1,000 District 2 40% 400
001 B 500 District 1 100% 500

We sum alloc_pop across blocks for each district. District 1 gets 600 + 500 = 1,100. District 2 gets 400. District 1 “wins” because it serves more people, and the tract inherits District 1’s spending data.

Occasionally two or more districts have identical alloc_pop values for the same tract. We need a tiebreaker. The code uses gsort tract70 sdtc -alloc_pop LEAID, which sorts by tract, then district type, then population (descending), and finally LEAID (ascending). In tie situations, the district with the lower LEAID value wins. In the current data, this occurs only 12 times.

Note on sdtc: The school district type code (sdtc) classifies districts by the grade levels they serve: 1 = unified (K-12), 2 = elementary only, 3 = secondary only, 4 = vocational. This classification matters because a single tract may be served by separate elementary and secondary districts.

2.2.3 Step 2: Tract-District Assignment

With allocated populations computed, we can now assign each tract to a single district. This involves three operations.

First, we collapse block-level alloc_pop to the tract × district × type level. This aggregates the block-level data up to tracts while preserving district type distinctions.

Second, we drop vocational districts (sdtc = 4). These are not relevant for K-12 analysis and would complicate the tract assignment.

Third, we pick the winner. For each tract-type combination, we keep only the district with the highest alloc_pop. This gives us a clean one-to-one mapping from tracts to districts.

2.2.4 Propagating Quality Flags

We also need to propagate district-level quality flags to tracts. We use a conservative rule: a tract is “good” for a given baseline year only if ALL districts serving it have valid data for that year.

There are two types of quality flags. Year-specific flags (good_tract_YYYY) indicate whether the tract has valid spending data for a specific baseline year. For example, good_tract_1967 = 1 means all districts serving this tract have valid 1967 spending data. The formula is:

\[ \text{good\_tract\_YYYY} = \min(\text{good\_govid\_YYYY}) \]

across all districts in the tract.

The composite flag (good_tract) indicates whether the tract has valid data for ALL baseline years (1967, 1970, 1971, and 1972). A tract is “good” only if all four year-specific flags equal 1. The formula is:

\[ \text{good\_tract} = \min(\text{good\_tract\_1967}, \text{good\_tract\_1970}, \text{good\_tract\_1971}, \text{good\_tract\_1972}) \]

Why take the minimum? Consider a tract served by three districts: A, B, and C. Even if we assign the tract’s spending to District A (the population winner), Districts B and C also serve students in this tract. If District B lacks 1970 spending data, the tract’s true 1970 spending picture is incomplete; we only observe what District A spent, not what Districts B and C spent. By taking the minimum, we flag such tracts as problematic.

This conservative approach means we may exclude some tracts that would otherwise have usable data. But it ensures that tracts flagged as “good” have unambiguous, complete spending histories.

2.2.5 Step 3: Final Merge

The final step joins district-year spending to tracts. We use joinby LEAID to link the tract-district crosswalk with the district panel from Stage 1. This expands one district-year row into multiple tract-year rows, since the same district can be assigned to multiple tracts. We then merge in the quality flags computed above.

The result is a tract-year panel where each tract has spending data inherited from its assigned district, along with quality flags indicating baseline data completeness.

3 Stage 3: County Quality Tagging

Stage 2 produced a tract-year panel with quality flags indicating baseline data completeness. Stage 3 aggregates these flags to the county level. The event-study design requires baseline data to construct spending quartiles, so we need to identify which counties have complete coverage.

3.1 Key Operation: Conservative Collapse

We collapse tract-level quality flags to counties using the minimum. The logic mirrors the tract-level approach: a county is “good” only if ALL its tracts are “good.”

\[ \text{good\_county\_YYYY} = \min(\text{good\_tract\_YYYY}) \]

across all tracts in the county. This produces two types of county quality flags.

Year-specific flags (good_county_YYYY) indicate whether the county has valid spending data for a specific baseline year. For example, good_county_1970 = 1 means every tract in this county has valid 1970 spending data. If even one tract in the county lacks data for that year, the flag equals zero.

Composite flags combine multiple years. The strictest is good_county, which equals 1 only if the county has valid data for all four baseline years (1967, 1970, 1971, and 1972). We also create good_county_6771 (valid for 1967, 1970, and 1971) and good_county_7072 (valid for 1970, 1971, and 1972). These alternatives provide flexibility: a county missing 1967 data but with complete 1970–1972 coverage can still be included in analyses that use a 1971 baseline.

Quality flags propagate through three levels:

  1. District level: good_govid_YYYY = 1 if the district has valid spending data for year YYYY

  2. Tract level: good_tract_YYYY = min(good_govid_YYYY) across all districts serving the tract

  3. County level: good_county_YYYY = min(good_tract_YYYY) across all tracts in the county

At each level, the MIN operation ensures that a single missing observation flags the entire unit as problematic.

With county quality tags in place, Stage 4 can construct the final county-year panel by aggregating tract spending, merging reform timing data, and applying sample restrictions.

4 Stage 4: County Panel Construction

Stage 3 produced county-level quality tags indicating which counties have complete baseline data. Stage 4 transforms the tract panel into a county-year analysis panel. This is the most complex stage, involving four substages: interpolation and tract assignment (4.1), inflation adjustment and aggregation (4.2), reform data merge (4.3), and income data merge (4.4). Each substage builds on the previous one, culminating in county_panel.

Why interpolation happens in Stage 4, not Stage 1: We delay interpolation until after quality tagging (Stage 3) to preserve the distinction between observed and imputed data. The quality flags must reflect actual data availability, not interpolated values. Only after tagging do we fill small gaps to maximize coverage for analysis.

4.1 Stage 4.1: District Interpolation and Tract Assignment

Before we can aggregate tracts to counties, we need to address gaps in the district spending data. The district panel contains missing values where spending data was not reported for certain years. This substage fills small gaps using linear interpolation, reassigns districts to tracts, and classifies counties by their tract composition.

4.1.1 Source Datasets

Dataset Purpose
dist_panel.dta District spending (Stage 1)
xwalk_tract_dist.dta Tract-district crosswalk (Stage 2)
grf_id_tractlevel.dta Tract metadata with no_tract indicator

4.1.2 Part 1: District Interpolation

The district panel contains gaps where spending data is missing for certain years. We fill gaps of three years or fewer using linear interpolation. Larger gaps risk too much measurement error, so we leave them unfilled.

The process works as follows. First, we compute the gap size between consecutive observations for each district. Second, we flag observations where the next available year is more than three years away. Third, we apply linear interpolation only within the acceptable gap range. This ensures we never extrapolate across long periods of missing data.

Process:

  1. Compute gap size: gap_next = year[_n+1] - year

  2. Flag large gaps: too_far = 1 if gap_next > 3

  3. Interpolate: ipolate pp_exp year if too_far == 0

4.1.3 Part 2: Tract Assignment

After interpolation, we repeat the tract assignment process from Stage 2. We load the tract-district crosswalk, pick the highest-population LEAID per tract, and join the interpolated spending data to tracts. This gives us a tract-year panel with more complete coverage than before interpolation.

4.1.4 Part 3: County Classification

Rural or sparsely populated areas lacked Census tract codes in 1970. The Census Bureau did not define tract boundaries for these “untracted areas,” so the GRF records them differently than tracted areas. We must handle untracted areas carefully to avoid mismeasuring county populations and spending.

We classify counties into four types based on their tract composition.

Type 1 counties are entirely tracted. These are counties where tract boundaries are well-defined. We process them using standard aggregation: sum tract populations and compute weighted average spending.

Type 2 counties contain only a single untracted area. These are counties where the Census defined no tracts. The GRF provides a single “untracted” record for the whole county, and we use county-level population data directly.

Type 3 counties contain multiple untracted areas (two or more). Some counties have multiple distinct “untracted” areas recorded separately in the GRF. This creates ambiguity, since we cannot determine how to distribute county population across these records. We average spending across the untracted units.

Type 4 counties are mixed, containing both tracted and untracted areas. The sum of tract populations does not equal the total county population. We compute the residual population and assign it to the untracted area: untracted_pop = county_total - sum(tract_populations).

With interpolated spending data and county classifications in place, we can proceed to Stage 4.2 to adjust for inflation and aggregate tracts to counties.

2 Single untracted only Treat the entire county as one unit
3 Multiple untracted (≥2) Ambiguous boundaries; average spending across units
4 Mixed (1 untracted + tracts) Compute residual population for untracted area

4.2 Stage 4.2: Inflation Adjustment and School-Age Population

Stage 4.1 produced a tract panel with interpolated spending in nominal dollars. Stage 4.2 converts spending to constant 2000 dollars, imports school-age population weights, and collapses tracts to counties. The result is a county-year panel with real per-pupil expenditure.

Dataset Purpose

4.2.2 Step 1: Inflation Adjustment

We convert nominal spending to constant 2000 dollars using the CPI-U (Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers) from FRED. The formula is:

The formula:

\[ \text{pp\_exp\_real} = \text{pp\_exp} \times \frac{\text{CPI}_{2000}}{\text{CPI}_{FY}} \]

where: - \(\text{pp\_exp}\) = nominal per-pupil expenditure in the given fiscal year - \(\text{CPI}_{2000}\) = CPI-U value for calendar year 2000 (our base year) - \(\text{CPI}_{FY}\) = average CPI-U value for the state’s fiscal year

The adjustment accounts for state-specific fiscal year calendars. School districts report spending by fiscal year, not calendar year, and fiscal year definitions vary by state. Most states use July 1 through June 30, but some use October 1 through September 30 or other calendars. We compute the CPI for each state’s fiscal year as the average CPI across the months in that fiscal year.

4.2.3 Step 2: School-Age Population Import

School-age population (ages 5–17) serves as the weighting variable for county aggregation. We import tract-level population from NHGIS, summing the variables that capture all children ages 5–17 regardless of enrollment status.

4.2.4 Step 3: Handling Tracted vs. Untracted Areas

Untracted areas lack tract-level Census data, so we must handle them separately. We split the data by the no_tract indicator: tracted areas merge with tract-level population data, while untracted areas merge with county-level population data. We then append the two pieces back together.

4.2.5 Step 4: Reshape and Assign Tract-Level PPE (Per-Pupil Expenditure)

Some tracts are served by multiple district types (for example, separate elementary and secondary districts). We reshape the data wide by district type to handle these cases. For tracts with multiple district types, we compute a weighted average based on the proportion of school-age children in each grade range. If data is missing for one district type, we fall back to the available single-district value.

\[ \text{ppe\_tract} = \text{pp\_exp\_real2} \times \text{share\_primary} + \text{pp\_exp\_real3} \times \text{share\_secondary} \]

4.2.6 Step 5: Handle Mixed Counties

For Type 4 counties (those with both tracted and untracted areas), the sum of tract populations does not equal the county total. We compute the residual population and assign it to the untracted row. This avoids double-counting when we aggregate to counties.

\[ \text{residual\_pop} = \text{county\_total} - \sum(\text{tract\_populations}) \]

4.2.7 Step 6: Collapse to Counties

Finally, we aggregate tract-level spending to counties using a population-weighted average. The weighting ensures that tracts with more school-age children contribute more to the county average.

The formula:

\[ \text{county\_exp} = \frac{\sum_{t \in \text{county}} \text{pp\_exp\_real}_t \times \text{school\_age\_pop}_t}{\sum_{t \in \text{county}} \text{school\_age\_pop}_t} \]

where: - \(t\) indexes tracts within the county - \(\text{pp\_exp\_real}_t\) = inflation-adjusted per-pupil expenditure for tract \(t\) - \(\text{school\_age\_pop}_t\) = school-age population (ages 5-17) in tract \(t\)

With inflation-adjusted county-level spending in hand, we can now merge the reform treatment data in Stage 4.3.

The Stata command:

collapse (mean) pp_exp_real [w = school_age_pop], by(county year4)

This produces county_exp (weighted average PPE) and school_age_pop (total) for each county-year.

Source: tabula-tabled2.xlsx (Table D2 from the JJP 2014 NBER Working Paper 20118, extracted via Tabula)

4.3.1 Understanding School Finance Reforms

School finance reforms occur when courts rule that a state’s school funding system violates the state constitution, typically by providing unequal educational opportunities across rich and poor districts. Following such rulings, states implement various reform mechanisms to equalize or increase school funding.

JJP compiled a comprehensive dataset of these court cases and their outcomes. Table D2 (from the 2014 working paper) records the court case name and year, the year the ruling took effect (which may differ from the ruling year), and the reform types implemented after the ruling.

4.3.2 Step 1: Processing the Reform Table

The JJP table contains multiple court cases for some states (if the original ruling was appealed, modified, or followed by additional litigation). We take the first court overturn as the treatment date because:

  1. The initial ruling establishes the legal precedent that forces legislative action.
  2. Later rulings typically build on or modify the initial reform, not replace it.
  3. Using the first ruling provides a consistent definition across states.

The processing involves several steps. First, we import the Excel file. The JJP table is a PDF that we converted to Excel using Tabula. Second, we parse the reform year, extracting the first year the court ruled the system unconstitutional. Third, we keep only the first overturn per state. Fourth, we create reform type flags.

JJP categorizes reforms into five types: minimum foundation plans (MFP), equalization plans (EP), local effort (LE), spending limits (SL), and equity cases (EQ). Reforms are not mutually exclusive. States without court overturns during our study period get reform_year = 0 and never_treated = 1. These states serve as the control group in our event-study design.

5 Stage 5: Sample Restrictions and Final Dataset

Stage 4 produced a county-year panel with spending, reform treatment, and income data. Stage 5 transforms this into the final analysis dataset. This stage performs three critical functions: creating the spending variables used in regressions, constructing event-time indicators for the event-study design, and enforcing balanced panel requirements.

Why do these steps matter? The event-study design requires consistent observation of units before and after treatment. If a county enters or exits our data during the event window, we cannot distinguish treatment effects from compositional changes. The restrictions in Stage 5 ensure that our estimates reflect actual spending responses to reform, not data artifacts.

5.1 Stage 5.1 Build analysis_panel_bal.dta (Primary Balance)

This substage produces the primary analysis dataset with strict balance requirements. The inputs are county_panel.dta from Stage 4.4 and county_qual_flags.dta from Stage 3.

5.1.1 Step 1: Creating Analysis Variables

The first step creates the spending variables we will use in regressions. We construct four versions, each with different properties.

The base variable exp is per-pupil expenditure winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles by year. Winsorizing replaces extreme values with the percentile cutoffs, bounding the influence of outliers without dropping observations. Raw per-pupil expenditure contains extreme values, some from data errors, others from unusual circumstances like a tiny district with very high spending.

From this we compute lexp, the log of winsorized spending. The log transformation enables percentage interpretation: a coefficient of 0.05 means approximately a 5% increase in spending.

We then compute lexp_ma, the log of a 13-year rolling mean. School spending fluctuates year-to-year due to budget cycles, one-time expenditures, and accounting timing. The rolling mean smooths this noise by averaging spending from year \(t-12\) through year \(t\). This choice also mirrors JJP’s use of average spending over ages 5–17.

Finally, lexp_ma_strict is the log of the rolling mean computed only when all 13 years have data. The regular rolling mean uses whatever years are available; the strict version equals missing if any of the 13 years lacks data. This ensures counties with data gaps are excluded rather than measured with error.

Variable Definition Use Case
exp Winsorized PPE (1st/99th percentile by year) Removes extreme outliers
lexp Log of winsorized PPE Regression outcome (single-year)
lexp_ma Log of 13-year rolling mean Smoothed outcome
lexp_ma_strict Log of rolling mean, only if full 13-year window exists Primary outcome

\[ \text{rollingmean}_t = \frac{1}{13} \sum_{j=0}^{12} \text{exp}_{t-j} \]

5.1.2 Step 2: Event-Time Indicators

Event-study regressions use “event time” (years measured relative to the treatment date) rather than calendar time. We calculate relative year as calendar year minus reform year. For example, if Texas reformed in 1989, then 1987 is relative year -2 (two years before reform), 1989 is relative year 0 (the reform year), and 1995 is relative year 6 (six years after reform). For never-treated states, we set relative year to missing since they have no reform year.

We then create indicator variables for each event-time period. The leads capture pre-reform years: lead_5 equals 1 if relative year is -5 or earlier (binned), lead_4 through lead_1 capture years -4 through -1 individually. The lags capture post-reform years: lag_1 through lag_16 capture years 1 through 16 individually, and lag_17 equals 1 if relative year is 17 or later (binned).

Year 0 (the reform year) is omitted as the reference category. All coefficients therefore measure changes relative to spending in the reform year. A coefficient of 0.10 on lag_5 means spending was 10% higher five years after reform compared to the reform year itself.

Calculating relative year:

\[ \text{relative\_year} = \text{calendar\_year} - \text{reform\_year} \]

5.1.3 Step 3: Baseline Quartiles

We classify counties by their pre-reform spending levels to test whether reforms affected low-spending and high-spending areas differently. Quartiles are computed within each state using 1971 (fiscal year 1972) data. For each state, we rank counties by their 1971 per-pupil expenditure and divide into four groups: Q1 (lowest 25%), Q2 (25th–50th percentile), Q3 (50th–75th percentile), and Q4 (highest 25%).

Why 1971? We need a baseline year that predates most reforms (most court cases occurred in the late 1970s onward), has good data coverage, and is close enough to reforms to reflect the pre-reform status quo. The year 1971 satisfies all three criteria.

We also compute income quartiles (inc_q) using 1970 Census median family income. This allows us to examine whether reforms helped low-income or high-income areas.

5.1.4 Step 4: Balanced Panel Restriction

The event-study design requires observing each county continuously across the event window. A county that appears and disappears cannot reliably identify treatment effects; changes in spending might reflect the county entering or exiting the data rather than responding to reform.

A county is “balanced” if lexp_ma_strict is non-missing for all 23 years of the event window, from relative year -5 through relative year +17. Why 23 years? The window spans -5, -4, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, …, 17, which totals 23 years.

This restriction substantially reduces sample size but ensures clean identification. Counties that fail the balance criterion often have data gaps from missing district-level finance data, from districts that formed or dissolved during the study period, or from incomplete Census tract coverage.

One important caveat: this approach excludes many states with early reforms, since those reforms occur less than 13 years after the first year of available data (fiscal year 1967). The strict rolling mean requires 13 prior years of data, which mechanically excludes early-reforming states from the balanced sample.

\[ \text{balanced} = 1 \iff \text{lexp\_ma\_strict}_{it} \neq \text{missing} \quad \forall t \in [-5, +17] \]

Example:

County Years with lexp_ma_strict Balanced?
Adams (CA) -5 to +17 (all 23) Yes
Baker (TX) -5 to +12 (18 years) No, missing +13 to +17
Clark (NY) -2 to +17 (missing early years) No, missing -5 to -3

5.1.5 Step 5: State Filter

Some states have very few counties in our final sample, perhaps only 2 or 3. Computing within-state quartiles with so few observations produces unstable estimates. We flag states with sufficient data:

  • valid_st = 1 if the state has ≥10 counties in the sample
  • valid_st_gd = 1 if the state has ≥10 “good” counties (with complete baseline data)

States failing this criterion remain in the dataset but are excluded from analyses that require within-state quartile comparisons.

These regressions estimate how school spending changed in response to court-ordered finance reforms, and whether those changes differed between initially low-spending and high-spending counties.

Source: analysis_panel_bal.dta or analysis_panel_bal_alt.dta (from Stage 5)

6.1 Regression Specification

The event-study design estimates spending trajectories before and after reform by regressing log per-pupil expenditure on a set of event-time indicators. The model takes the form:

lexp_ma_strict = α + Σ βₖ·lag_k + Σ γₖ·lead_k + δ·year + county_FE + ε
Component Implementation
Outcome lexp, lexp_ma, or lexp_ma_strict
Fixed effects County (absorb(county_id)) + Year (i.year)
Clustering County-level (vce(cluster county_id))
Weights School-age population ([w=school_age_pop])
Sample good==1, valid_st_gd==1, reform_year<2000

The outcome variable is the log of the 13-year rolling mean of per-pupil expenditure, though we also estimate specifications using single-year log spending (lexp) and the non-strict rolling mean (lexp_ma). The key regressors are the lead and lag indicators constructed in Stage 5, which capture spending in each year relative to the reform date. Year 0 (the reform year) is omitted as the reference category, so all coefficients measure changes relative to spending in the reform year.

The model includes county fixed effects (absorbed via areg) and year fixed effects. County fixed effects control for time-invariant differences across counties, while year fixed effects control for national trends in school spending that affect all counties equally. Standard errors are clustered at the county level to account for correlation within counties over time. Observations are weighted by school-age population so that larger counties contribute more to the estimates.

The sample restrictions ensure we include only counties with complete baseline data (good==1), in states with at least 10 valid counties (valid_st_gd==1), and with reform dates early enough to observe post-reform outcomes (reform_year<2000).

6.2 Analysis Structure

By Quartile. We run separate regressions for each baseline spending quartile (Q1=poorest to Q4=richest). This reveals whether reforms differentially affected poor vs. rich districts.

Pooled Q1-Q3. We pool the bottom three quartiles to test overall effects on non-wealthy districts.
